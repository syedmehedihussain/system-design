1. Object-Oriented Analysis & UML

LECTURE 7 · CHAPTER 10 · ~40 MARKS

  • Core OO: an object is a person, place or thing with attributes and behaviours; a class is the shared definition, drawn as a 3-compartment rectangle (name / attributes / methods); inheritance passes both down to derived classes.
  • Building blocks: things (structural, behavioural, group, annotational), relationships, and diagrams split into structural (class, object, component, deployment) and behavioural (use case, sequence, communication, statechart, activity).
  • Use case: what the system does, never how. Boundary rectangle, actors outside as stick figures, verb + noun ovals inside, plus <<include>> for always and <<extend>> for sometimes.
  • Activity: swimlane per participant, rounded boxes for actions, diamonds for decisions with guards in brackets, fork and join bars for concurrency.
  • Sequence: time flows downward. Actor → boundary → control → entity → external system, with activation bars and a dashed labelled return for every request.
  • Class: static only. Visibility symbols, multiplicity, hollow triangle for inheritance, hollow diamond for aggregation, filled diamond for composition, association class to break many-to-many.
In the examDraw a use case diagram with actors labelled primary or supporting, write a use case narration in three sections, draw an activity diagram for one named process, a sequence diagram for one scenario, and a class diagram with generalization.
Costs marksMissing multiplicity at both ends, unlabelled decision branches, skipped return messages, and forgetting to mark which actors are supporting.

2. Data Flow Diagrams + CRUD Matrix

LECTURE 8 · CHAPTER 7 · ~20 MARKS

  • Four symbols: double square = external entity (noun), arrow = data flow (noun), rounded rectangle = process (verb + adjective + noun), open-ended rectangle = data store (noun).
  • Context diagram: exactly one process numbered 0, every external entity, no data stores at all.
  • Diagram 0: the explosion of that process into at most nine numbered processes. Data stores appear here for the first time.
  • Child diagrams: process 4 explodes into 4.1, 4.2, 4.3. Balancing means the child cannot take in or send out anything the parent does not.
  • Logical vs physical: logical describes the business and its events; physical adds hardware, people, manual steps, real file names, sequencing and controls.
  • CRUD matrix: processes as rows, data stores or entities as columns. Read it straight off your DFD, since a flow into a store is C or U and a flow out is R.
  • Event response table: source and destination become entities, trigger and response become flows, activity becomes a process, and any "db" inside the activity is a data store.
In the examProduce all three DFD levels from an event response table, then a CRUD matrix "according to your DFD", with a few summary lines underneath.
Costs marksConnecting a data store straight to an external entity, black hole processes with no output, more than nine processes on one sheet, and a child whose boundary does not match its parent.

3. Process Specs & Structured Decisions

LECTURE 9 · LIKELY MCQ OR SHORT QUESTION

  • Minispecs are written for primitive processes to remove ambiguity. They are not written for physical input/output, simple validation, or prewritten code.
  • Structured English: use it for formulas, iteration, and decisions that are not complex. Keywords are IF, THEN, ELSE, DO, DO WHILE, DO UNTIL, PERFORM.
  • Decision table: four quadrants (conditions, condition alternatives, actions, action entries). Columns = alternatives raised to the number of conditions, so three yes/no conditions give eight rules.
  • Fill alternatives systematically: first condition half Y then half N, then halve again each row. That guarantees no combination is missed.
  • Check for four problems: incompleteness, impossible situations, contradictions and redundancy. Merge columns with a dash when a condition stops mattering.
  • Decision tree: branch left to right, one condition per node, an action at every leaf. Use it when the order of checking matters.
In the examMostly MCQ: which technique fits which situation, what a minispec contains, and how many rule columns a decision table needs.
Remember the ruleStructured English when communicating with users, decision tables for complex condition combinations and completeness, decision trees when sequence matters.

4. Designing Effective Output

LECTURE 10 · CHAPTER 11 · ~10 MARKS

  • Six objectives: output should serve a specific purpose, be meaningful to the user, arrive in the right quantity, in the right place, on time, and by the right method.
  • Forms of output: hard copy and soft copy. External output leaves the organisation (bills, paychecks) and often needs preprinted forms; internal output stays inside (summary, detailed, historical, exception reports).
  • Report types: detailed gives a line per record, exception only shows records meeting a condition, summary gives totals per group.
  • Output bias creeps in through how information is sorted, where acceptable limits are set, and the choice of graphics such as a truncated axis.
  • Display and dashboards: keep it simple, keep presentation consistent, give data context, and use well-designed labels and legends.
In the examGiven a data table, draw the right chart. Autumn 2024 gave 6 marks for the data points and 1 mark each for axis labels, title and series.
Costs marksSketching a few bars and stopping, leaving an axis unlabelled, or omitting the legend. Start the y-axis at zero so the graph is not biased.

5. Designing Effective Input

LECTURE 11 · CHAPTER 12 · ~10 MARKS

  • Four guidelines: a form should be easy to fill in, meet its purpose, ensure accurate completion, and be attractive. Input quality decides output quality.
  • Seven sections in order: heading, identification and access, instructions, body, signature and verification, totals, comments. Form flow runs left to right, top to bottom.
  • Match the control to the data: text box for free entry, check box for many choices, radio button for one, drop-down when space is tight, text area for long text, slider or spin button for a range, message box for warnings, command button for actions.
  • Three-clicks rule: users should reach the screen they need within three clicks or keystrokes.
  • Colour: the five most legible combinations are black on yellow, green on white, blue on white, white on blue, and yellow on black.
  • Web fill-in forms need clear instructions, a logical entry sequence, and the two basic buttons Submit and Clear.
In the examGiven a table of fields, draw the input form. Autumn 2024 marked it at 1 mark per feature, so every field, caption and button counts.
Costs marksLeaving out ADD, EDIT, SAVE, DELETE, CLOSE and the navigation buttons, an unlabelled control, or no grid area for repeating line items.

Last-minute checklist

Read this on the way in. Every line is a place the marking scheme awards or removes a mark.

  • Use case: box the system boundary and name it, stick figures outside, verb-phrase ovals inside, label primary versus supporting actors.
  • Narration: three sections, actor action and system response in two numbered columns, preconditions and postconditions at the end.
  • Activity: filled circle to start, rounded boxes for actions, diamonds for decisions with bracketed guards, bars for fork and join, bullseye to end, one swimlane per participant.
  • Sequence: actor first, dashed lifelines, activation bars, a dashed return for every message, time flowing downward.
  • Class: three compartments, visibility symbols, multiplicity at both ends, hollow triangle for inheritance, diamonds for aggregation and composition.
  • DFD: context is one process numbered 0 with no data stores, level 1 has at most nine processes, level 2 numbers after its parent, never join a store directly to an entity, balance with the parent.
  • CRUD: processes as rows, stores or entities as columns, one or more of C, R, U, D per cell, blanks where there is no interaction.
  • Input form: every field with the right control and a caption, plus ADD, EDIT, SAVE, DELETE, CLOSE and navigation.
  • Output chart: title, both axis labels, series legend, and every data point plotted.
  • Project question: three sentences on the project, three on the prototype, one justification.
